01 · The basics

What is celiac disease?

It’s more than avoiding a food. It’s an autoimmune condition that needs a proper diagnosis and ongoing care.

Celiac disease is a chronic autoimmune condition. When a person with celiac disease eats gluten, their immune system reacts and can damage the lining of the small intestine. Over time, that damage may make it harder for the body to absorb nutrients.

Gluten is a group of proteins found in wheat, barley, and rye. Celiac disease is not a food preference, a wheat allergy, or the same thing as non-celiac gluten sensitivity.

It can affect people at any age. Symptoms vary widely, and some people may have few obvious symptoms. A clinician can help decide whether evaluation is appropriate.

Key idea: Symptoms alone can’t confirm celiac disease. Diagnosis requires medical evaluation and appropriate testing.

02 · Symptoms

There isn’t one “celiac look.”

Symptoms can be digestive, non-digestive, subtle, or absent. They can also overlap with many other health conditions.

01

Digestive changes

Diarrhea, constipation, bloating, gas, abdominal pain, nausea, or vomiting may occur.

02

Energy & nutrition

Fatigue, iron-deficiency anemia, or difficulty maintaining weight can be associated with celiac disease.

03

Skin & other signs

An itchy rash, bone or joint concerns, mouth symptoms, or growth concerns in children may occur.

These symptoms have many possible causes. This list is not a checklist for diagnosing yourself. If something concerns you, talk with a healthcare professional.

03 · Why it happens

Genes matter, but they aren’t the whole story.

Celiac disease involves gluten, the immune system, and genetic susceptibility. Having risk-associated genes does not mean someone will definitely develop it.

Many people with celiac disease have gene variants commonly called HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8. These variants are common in the general population, while only a portion of people who carry them develop celiac disease. Genes are one part of the picture, not a diagnosis.

Celiac disease is different from wheat allergy, which is an allergic reaction to wheat, and from non-celiac gluten sensitivity, which can cause symptoms after gluten exposure but does not cause the same intestinal injury as celiac disease.

Three different things

Celiac disease: an autoimmune condition triggered by gluten.

Wheat allergy: an allergic response to proteins in wheat.

Non-celiac gluten sensitivity: symptoms linked to gluten or wheat without celiac disease or wheat allergy.

04 · Diagnosis

Get tested before changing your diet.

Evaluation is a conversation with a clinician—not a result from symptoms, an online quiz, or a home experiment.

01

Talk through your history

A healthcare professional can review symptoms, family history, and health context, then discuss whether testing makes sense.

02

Blood tests may come first

Celiac antibody tests are commonly part of evaluation. The clinician chooses and interprets tests based on the person’s situation.

03

Follow-up testing if needed

An upper endoscopy with small-intestine biopsies may be used. The approach can vary, including for children.

If you already stopped eating gluten: tell your clinician. They can help discuss options; don’t restart or change your diet solely because of this website.

05 · Treatment & support

Care is more than a grocery list.

For people diagnosed with celiac disease, treatment generally involves a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet, guided by qualified health professionals.

A physician and a registered dietitian with celiac experience can help create a balanced eating plan, understand food labels, and navigate meals away from home. Follow-up care can help monitor health and address nutritional needs.

Packaged foods, restaurant meals, shared kitchens, and some medicines or supplements can raise questions about ingredients or cross-contact. Specific advice depends on the product, local labeling rules, and personal medical needs.

Be kind to yourself while learning. Getting support from a trusted clinician, dietitian, family member, or reputable celiac organization can make practical changes feel more manageable.

Helpful first steps

  • Talk to a healthcare professional before changing your diet.
  • Ask whether a celiac-experienced dietitian can support you.
  • Learn how to check ingredient labels in your location.
  • Ask about cross-contact for your own circumstances.

06 · Everyday life

Small questions, useful conversations.

Living gluten-free can look different from one person or household to another. These prompts can help you get clear, personal guidance.

At home

Ask about cross-contact

Discuss shared preparation areas, utensils, and appliances with a dietitian to find practical steps that fit your household.

Eating out

Make your needs clear

Ask a restaurant about ingredients and how food is prepared. Menus and practices vary, so confirm directly.

At school

Plan with trusted adults

Families can talk with school staff and healthcare professionals about safe, inclusive meal arrangements for a student.

These are conversation starters, not a complete safety checklist. Follow advice tailored to your diagnosis and local food-labeling guidance.

07 · Quick answers

A few common questions.

Short answers for learning. A healthcare professional can help with individual decisions.

Should I stop eating gluten before a test?

Don’t change your diet before testing unless your healthcare professional advises you to. Removing gluten can make test results less reliable.

Can someone have celiac disease without obvious symptoms?

Yes. Some people have few or no noticeable symptoms. A clinician can advise whether testing is appropriate based on personal and family health history.

Is a gluten-free diet a cure?

No. It is the established treatment for celiac disease, and ongoing professional follow-up can be helpful.

Can I use this guide to diagnose myself?

No. Symptoms overlap with other conditions, and diagnosis needs appropriate clinical evaluation and testing.

Is celiac disease the same as a wheat allergy?

No. Celiac disease is an autoimmune condition; wheat allergy is an allergic reaction. A clinician can help distinguish causes of symptoms.
