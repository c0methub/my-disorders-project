What happens when gluten meets an immune system that reacts to it? Here’s a clear, approachable guide to celiac disease—what it is, how it’s identified, and what treatment really means.

01 · The basics

So, what is celiac disease?

It’s a long-term autoimmune condition where eating gluten triggers an immune response that can damage the small intestine.

What gluten is

Gluten is a group of proteins found in wheat, barley, and rye.

What it does

It can injure the intestine and interfere with nutrient absorption.

What it is not

Not the same as a wheat allergy or non-celiac gluten sensitivity.

02 · Symptoms

Symptoms don’t follow one script.

Symptoms can be digestive, non-digestive, subtle, or absent. These are examples, not a checklist.

Digestive

Diarrhea, bloating, constipation, gas, nausea, or abdominal discomfort.

Energy & growth

Fatigue, low iron, difficulty keeping weight, or slowed growth in kids.

Other signs

Itchy rash, bone issues, or unexplained changes in health.

03 · Why it happens

Gluten is the trigger; biology matters too.

Celiac disease involves an immune response to gluten and genetic susceptibility. It is not contagious.

Genes matter: certain genes are associated with celiac disease, but genes alone do not decide the outcome.

Key point: a reaction to gluten is not the same as preferring a gluten-free lifestyle.

04 · Diagnosis

Diagnosis takes proper testing.

A healthcare professional may order blood tests and sometimes an endoscopy to look at the small intestine.

Review history

Doctors look at symptoms, family history, and other health questions.

Blood tests

Testing often begins with celiac-related antibodies.

Biopsy if needed

An endoscopy can help confirm the diagnosis.

Do not stop eating gluten before testing unless a clinician tells you to. Gluten-free eating before testing can make results less reliable.

05 · Treatment

There isn’t a cure yet. There is treatment.

The established treatment is a strict lifelong gluten-free diet, with support from a healthcare team.

Food plan: avoid wheat, barley, and rye; read labels carefully.

Support helps: a clinician or registered dietitian can guide a balanced, realistic approach.
