A student guide · Health, explained with care

Celiac disease,
clearly.

What happens when gluten meets an immune system that reacts to it? Here’s a straightforward guide to celiac disease—what it is, how it’s found, and what treatment really means.

A closer
look
Gluten is found in wheat, barley and rye.Photo: Unsplash
Before testing, keep eating gluten unless a clinician tells you otherwise. Starting a gluten-free diet first can make test results less reliable. Talk to a healthcare professional if you think you may have celiac disease.

01 · The basics

So, what is celiac disease?

It’s a long-term autoimmune condition—not an allergy, and not simply a food preference.

In someone with celiac disease, eating gluten (a protein in wheat, barley and rye) triggers the immune system to attack the lining of the small intestine. Over time, that can damage tiny finger-like projections called villi. Villi help the body absorb nutrients from food.

That damage can cause digestive symptoms, affect other parts of the body, or cause no obvious symptoms. The condition runs in families and is associated with certain genes, but having those genes does not mean a person will definitely develop it.

Important distinction: Celiac disease is different from wheat allergy and non-celiac gluten sensitivity. A medical professional can help work out what is causing symptoms.

02 · How common?

More common than many people think.

Estimates depend on where and how researchers look. Many sources describe celiac disease as affecting about 1 in 100 people worldwide; some people have it without knowing.

03 · What it can feel like

Symptoms don’t follow one script.

Some people have digestive symptoms, some have symptoms elsewhere, and some don’t notice clear symptoms. This list is for learning—not self-diagnosis.

  • Diarrhea or constipation
  • Bloating or gas
  • Abdominal pain
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Fatigue
  • Iron-deficiency anemia
  • Unintended weight changes
  • Itchy skin rash
  • Bone or joint problems
  • Growth concerns in children

Why it can be missed

Symptoms can be mild, change over time, or resemble other conditions. A person can also have intestinal damage without obvious digestive symptoms.

If you’re concerned, ask a healthcare professional about testing.

04 · Why it happens

Gluten is the trigger; biology matters too.

Celiac disease develops through an immune response to gluten in people with a genetic susceptibility. It isn’t contagious.

Genes raise the chance—but don’t decide everything.

Celiac disease is associated with certain HLA-DQ genes. Many people carry these genes, but only a portion develop celiac disease. Researchers are still studying why.

Oats are naturally gluten-free, but can be contaminated during growing or processing; ask a clinician or dietitian what is appropriate for you.

05 · Finding out

Diagnosis takes proper testing.

A healthcare professional chooses tests based on symptoms and history. Testing is most reliable while the person is still eating gluten.

Blood tests

Doctors often start with celiac antibody tests, including tTG-IgA and total IgA.

Sometimes, a biopsy

An upper endoscopy may collect small-intestine samples for examination by a pathologist.

Put results together

A clinician interprets test results and history. A symptom checklist cannot confirm diagnosis.

What equipment might be involved?

Clinicians may use laboratory blood tests, an upper endoscope (a thin flexible camera), and pathology equipment to examine a biopsy sample. There is no special home device that diagnoses celiac disease.

Illustration is for learning; a clinician decides which tests are appropriate.

Important: don’t start a gluten-free diet before being evaluated unless your clinician advises it. Removing gluten can make tests harder to interpret. If you already stopped, tell your clinician.

06 · Treatment

There isn’t a cure yet. There is treatment.

The established treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet, planned with reliable guidance.

What treatment often involves

  • A dietitian or clinician familiar with celiac disease can help plan a balanced diet.
  • Learn to check labels and avoid cross-contact in kitchens and restaurants.
  • Follow-up care may check symptoms, nutrition, and intestinal healing.

About a “cure”: A gluten-free diet controls the condition; it does not remove the underlying tendency to react. Talk with a healthcare professional about care.
