Celiac disease,
clearly.

I wanted to explain the basics without making it complicated: what happens in the body, who may be more likely to get celiac disease, and how doctors check for it.

Made by Mo <3

One thing to know before testing

People being checked for celiac disease are usually told to keep eating gluten until their clinician says otherwise. Avoiding it beforehand can affect test results.

01 · Cause

What causes celiac disease?

Celiac disease is an autoimmune condition. Gluten—the protein found in wheat, barley, and rye—sets off an immune reaction in people who are susceptible to it.

The immune reaction

The immune system reacts to gluten and can damage the tiny, finger-like villi lining the small intestine. Villi help the body absorb nutrients from food.

Genes are part of it

Some gene variants make celiac disease more likely, but having them does not mean a person will definitely develop it. Other factors are involved, and researchers are still studying them.

It is not an allergy

Celiac disease is different from a wheat allergy or gluten sensitivity. It is not contagious; you cannot catch it from someone else.

02 · Prevalence

Who is more likely to have it?

Celiac disease can develop at any age and affects people around the world. The estimate often used is about 1 in 100 people worldwide, though rates and diagnosis vary from place to place.

Close relatives

Having a parent, sibling, or child with celiac disease raises a person’s chance of having it too. A clinician can advise whether testing makes sense.

Some health conditions

Celiac disease is more common in people with certain conditions, including type 1 diabetes and some autoimmune disorders. This is a risk link, not a diagnosis.

People with the genes

Most people with celiac disease have HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8 gene variants. These variants are common, though, and most people who have them do not get celiac disease.

Worth remembering: It can affect children or adults, and some people have few obvious symptoms. Risk factors alone cannot tell whether someone has it.

03 · Symptoms

It can show up in different ways.

Some people notice digestive problems. Others mainly feel run-down or have signs linked to low nutrient levels. Symptoms can also be mild or absent.

Stomach and gut

Bloating, belly pain, gas, diarrhea, constipation, nausea, or vomiting are possible. Not everyone has digestive symptoms.

Energy and nutrition

Feeling unusually tired, iron-deficiency anemia, or difficulty maintaining weight can happen when the intestine is not absorbing nutrients well.

Other signs

Some people have an itchy rash, bone or joint problems, or—among children—slower growth. These symptoms can have many causes.

Symptoms alone do not confirm celiac disease. A healthcare professional can decide what testing is appropriate.

04 · Cure / treatment

There is no cure yet, but it can be managed.

The main treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet. For most people with celiac disease, avoiding gluten allows symptoms to improve and the small intestine to heal.

Avoid gluten

This means avoiding wheat, barley, and rye, as well as foods made with them. Even small amounts from crumbs or shared cooking surfaces can matter, so preventing cross-contact is important.

Get practical support

A doctor and dietitian can help with food choices, label reading, eating away from home, and making sure the diet includes the nutrients a person needs.

Keep up with care

Follow-up visits can help check symptoms, nutrition, and whether the treatment plan is working. A clinician may recommend supplements if testing shows a deficiency.

Don’t start a gluten-free diet just to test yourself. Removing gluten before a medical evaluation can make test results harder to interpret. Ask a clinician for advice.

05 · Detection / equipment

How do doctors check for it?

Testing usually starts with blood work. Depending on the results and the person’s situation, a specialist may also look at the small intestine and take tiny tissue samples.

Blood test

A lab checks a blood sample for antibodies associated with celiac disease, often tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA), along with total IgA. The clinician chooses the right tests and interprets them together with the person’s health history.

Endoscopy and biopsy

An endoscope is a thin, flexible tube with a light and camera. A doctor guides it through the mouth to the upper small intestine, then uses tiny tools to collect tissue samples. A lab examines the samples for damage consistent with celiac disease.

Genetic testing

A blood or saliva test can look for HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8. It may help in particular situations, but a positive result cannot diagnose celiac disease because these variants are common in people who never develop it.

What about MRI? MRI is not a usual test for diagnosing celiac disease. An MRI scanner uses a strong magnetic field to line up some hydrogen atoms in the body, then sends radio waves that briefly change their alignment. As the atoms return to their usual state, they give off signals; the scanner and a computer use those signals to build images. MRI does not use X-rays, but celiac disease is generally checked with blood tests and, when needed, an endoscopy with biopsy—not an MRI.

Keep eating gluten before testing unless your clinician advises otherwise. A gluten-free diet can make blood tests and biopsies less reliable.
