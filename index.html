01 · The basics

So, what is celiac disease?

It’s a long-term autoimmune condition—not an allergy, and not simply a food preference.

In someone with celiac disease, eating gluten (a protein in wheat, barley and rye) triggers the immune system to attack the lining of the small intestine. Over time, that can damage tiny finger-like projections called villi. Villi help the body absorb nutrients from food.

That damage can cause digestive symptoms, but it can also affect other parts of the body—or cause no obvious symptoms at all. The condition runs in families and is associated with certain genes, but having those genes does not mean a person will definitely develop it.

Important distinction: Celiac disease is different from wheat allergy and non-celiac gluten sensitivity. A medical professional can help work out what is causing symptoms.

02 · How common?

More common than many people think.

Estimates depend on where and how researchers look. Many health organizations describe celiac disease as affecting about 1 in 100 people worldwide; some people have it without knowing.

03 · What it can feel like

Symptoms don’t follow one script.

Some people have digestive symptoms, some have symptoms elsewhere, and some don’t notice clear symptoms. This list is for learning—not self-diagnosis.

  • Diarrhea or constipation
  • Bloating or gas
  • Abdominal pain
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Fatigue
  • Iron-deficiency anemia
  • Unintended weight changes
  • Itchy skin rash in some people
  • Bone or joint problems
  • Growth concerns in children

Why it can be missed

Symptoms can be mild, change over time, or resemble other conditions. A person can also have intestinal damage without obvious digestive symptoms.

If you’re concerned, ask a healthcare professional about testing. Don’t use symptoms alone to decide whether you have celiac disease.

04 · Why it happens

Gluten is the trigger; biology matters too.

Celiac disease develops through an immune response to gluten in people with a genetic susceptibility. It isn’t caused by a lack of willpower, and it isn’t contagious.

Genes can raise the chance—but don’t decide everything.

Celiac disease is strongly associated with certain versions of the HLA-DQ genes. Many people carry these genes, but only a small portion develop celiac disease. Researchers are still studying why it develops in some people and not others.

Gluten is present in wheat, barley and rye. Oats are naturally gluten-free, but they can be contaminated during growing or processing; ask a clinician or dietitian about what is appropriate for you.

It isn’t caused by eating “too much” gluten, and it is not the same thing as an immediate wheat allergy.

05 · Finding out

Diagnosis takes proper testing.

A healthcare professional chooses tests based on the person’s symptoms and history. Testing is most reliable while the person is still eating gluten.

Blood tests

Doctors commonly start with celiac antibody tests, often tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA) along with a total IgA test. Other tests may be used in specific situations.

Sometimes, a biopsy

For many people, an upper endoscopy is used to take small samples from the small intestine. A pathologist examines them for changes consistent with celiac disease.

Put results together

Age, test results, symptoms, and clinical guidelines all matter. A clinician interprets the results; a home test or symptom checklist can’t confirm the diagnosis.

What equipment might be involved?

Diagnosis is about medical tests, not one special home device. Depending on the situation, clinicians may use:

  • Laboratory blood testing for celiac-related antibodies.
  • An upper endoscope, a thin flexible camera passed through the mouth during a procedure, to collect small-intestine biopsies when needed.
  • Laboratory pathology equipment to examine biopsy tissue under a microscope.

The drawing is illustrative. A clinician decides which tests are appropriate.

Please don’t start a gluten-free diet before being evaluated unless your clinician advises it. Removing gluten can make blood tests and biopsies harder to interpret. If you already stopped, tell your clinician rather than restarting on your own.

06 · Treatment

There isn’t a cure yet. There is treatment.

The established treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet, planned with reliable guidance. For most people, avoiding gluten allows the intestine to heal over time.

Food can still be joyful

What treatment often involves

  • A dietitian or clinician who understands celiac disease can help plan a nutritionally balanced gluten-free diet.
  • Learn how to check ingredient labels and avoid cross-contact in kitchens, restaurants, and food preparation.
  • Follow-up care may include checking symptoms, nutrition, and whether the intestine is healing.
  • Some people need treatment for nutrient deficiencies or other related health concerns.
About a “cure”: A gluten-free diet controls the condition; it does not remove the underlying tendency to react. Don’t stop treatment or take supplements as a substitute for medical advice.
