01 · Cause

What causes celiac disease?

Celiac disease is an autoimmune condition. When a person with celiac disease eats gluten, the immune system responds by damaging the small intestine.

Gluten is a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye. The reaction can reduce the surface area available for absorbing nutrients, even when symptoms are mild or not obvious.

⌁

The key difference

It is not a wheat allergy.

Celiac disease involves the immune system and intestinal injury. It is also different from a general gluten sensitivity.

02 · Prevalence

Who is more likely to have it?

Celiac disease affects people across every age and background.

01

Family history

First-degree relatives of a person with celiac disease have a higher chance of developing it.

02

Some genetic conditions

Risk is higher in people with certain conditions, including type 1 diabetes and Down syndrome.

03

Others can be affected

Anyone can develop celiac disease. It may begin in childhood or later in life.

A family history or additional risk factor does not mean a person definitely has celiac disease.

03 · Symptoms

It can look different in every person.

Some symptoms involve digestion. Others affect energy, the skin, bones, or general well-being.

01

Digestive changes

Diarrhea, stomach pain, bloating, or constipation may occur.

02

Low energy

Fatigue or anemia can happen when nutrients are not absorbed normally.

03

Skin or bone changes

Dermatitis herpetiformis and bone loss are possible manifestations.

Some people have very few symptoms, while others have no obvious digestive symptoms. Testing should be guided by a healthcare professional.

04 · Cure and treatment

There is no cure yet, but it can be managed.

The main treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet. It allows the small intestine to heal and prevents further immune damage.

♡

Be kind to the person managing it. Cross-contact with even small amounts of gluten can matter.

05 · Detection and equipment

How do doctors check for it?

Testing usually begins with blood tests. If results are positive or unclear, an endoscopic biopsy may be needed.

01

Review history

A clinician asks about symptoms, diet, family history, and related conditions.

02

Blood testing

Blood tests look for antibodies associated with celiac disease.

03

Possible biopsy

An endoscope lets a specialist examine the intestine and collect a tiny tissue sample.

Do not begin a gluten-free diet before testing. Speak with a healthcare professional and follow their instructions.
