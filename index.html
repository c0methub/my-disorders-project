Celiac disease,
clearly.

When gluten triggers the body in the wrong way, it can affect the gut and how the body takes in nutrients. Here’s a simple guide to what it is, what it can feel like, and what helps.

Start reading ↓
Wheat has gluten
✦ One condition.
Many experiences.
Before testing, keep eating gluten unless a clinician tells you otherwise. Stopping gluten too early can make test results less reliable. Talk to a healthcare professional if you think you might have celiac disease.

01 · Cause

What causes it?

Celiac disease happens when the body reacts to gluten. Gluten is a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye. This reaction can damage the lining of the small intestine over time.

In simple terms: the body mistakes gluten for a threat and attacks the gut by mistake.

It can run in families, but having a family link does not mean everyone will get it.

02 · How common

It affects people around the world.

About 1 in 100 people worldwide may have celiac disease. It can start at any age, and some people do not know they have it.

Any age

Children and adults can both develop celiac disease.

Easy to miss

Some people have mild signs or no clear symptoms.

Family history

A doctor may suggest testing if a close family member has it.

03 · Symptoms

It does not look the same for everyone.

Some people have gut problems. Others feel tired or notice changes in their skin, bones, or growth. These signs can have other causes too.

Gut problems

Stomach pain, bloating, diarrhea, constipation, nausea, or gas.

Energy and weight

Feeling tired, low on iron, or having trouble keeping weight on.

Other signs

Itchy skin rash, bone or joint problems, or slow growth in children.

Only a health professional can check what is causing symptoms.

04 · Cure / treatment

There is no cure yet, but treatment can help.

The usual treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet. That means avoiding wheat, barley, and rye, including foods made with them.

Get support: a doctor and dietitian can help with food choices and reading labels.

Follow-up care helps check that the diet is working and the body is getting what it needs.

Many people feel better when they follow the gluten-free plan made with their care team.

05 · Detection / equipment

Doctors use tests to check for it.

Testing often starts with a blood test. Sometimes a doctor uses a thin camera, called an endoscope, to look inside the small intestine and take a tiny sample.

Talk first

Share symptoms and family history with a doctor.

Blood test

A blood sample can look for signs linked to celiac disease.

Camera test, if needed

An endoscope can help the doctor look at the gut and take a small sample.

Keep eating gluten before testing unless your doctor tells you otherwise. Stopping can make test results harder to read.

06 · Creative presentation

Picture the gut like a soft brush.

The small intestine has tiny parts that help the body take in nutrients. In celiac disease, gluten can trigger a reaction that harms those parts.

Imagine: the gut is a soft brush that helps sweep nutrients into the body. Gluten sets off a mistaken alarm, and the body damages the brush. Treatment helps the gut heal by keeping gluten out of meals.
