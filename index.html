01 / THE BASICS

First things first:
what is celiac disease?

Celiac disease is a long-term autoimmune condition. When someone with celiac disease eats gluten—a protein in wheat, barley, and rye—their immune system reacts and can damage the lining of the small intestine.

That damage can make it harder for the body to absorb nutrients. Celiac disease isn’t a food preference, and it’s not the same as a wheat allergy or non-celiac gluten sensitivity.

In plain English: gluten triggers an immune response that can hurt the gut. A clinician can help figure out whether celiac disease is behind someone’s symptoms.

↗

A quick definition

It’s about the immune system—not willpower.

Having certain genes can raise the chance of celiac disease, but genes alone don’t tell the whole story.

02 / WHAT IT CAN FEEL LIKE

No single symptom tells
the whole story.

Some people notice gut problems. Others feel tired or have symptoms that seem unrelated. Some people don’t notice clear symptoms at all.

01

Digestive changes

Bloating, gas, stomach pain, diarrhea, constipation, nausea, or vomiting.

02

Energy & nutrition

Fatigue, iron-deficiency anemia, or unintended weight changes.

03

Other signs

Itchy skin rash, bone or joint problems, or growth concerns in children.

Symptoms can overlap with other conditions, so this list can’t diagnose celiac disease. If something concerns you, talk to a healthcare professional.

03 / WHY IT HAPPENS

Gluten is the trigger.
Biology matters too.

Celiac disease is linked to certain genes and an immune response to gluten. It isn’t contagious, and carrying the genes doesn’t mean a person will definitely develop the condition.

Oats are naturally gluten-free, but they can pick up gluten during growing or processing. A clinician or dietitian can help explain what’s safe for an individual.

04 / FINDING OUT

Answers start with
the right tests.

A healthcare professional considers symptoms and medical history, then recommends appropriate testing.

01

Blood tests

Testing often starts with celiac antibodies, including tTG-IgA and total IgA.

02

Sometimes, a biopsy

An upper endoscopy may collect small-intestine samples for examination.

03

Results in context

A clinician looks at test results alongside symptoms and health history.

Before your appointment: don’t remove gluten from your diet unless your clinician advises you to. If you already have, let them know.

05 / TREATMENT & EVERYDAY LIFE

With the right support,
life gets more manageable.

The established treatment is a lifelong gluten-free diet. Support from a healthcare professional or dietitian can make the changes easier to navigate.

01

Learn what to look for

Check ingredient labels and ask questions when buying food or eating out.

02

Watch for cross-contact

Crumbs and shared kitchen tools can matter. Small routines can help reduce accidental exposure.

03

Keep up with care

Follow-up appointments can help monitor symptoms, nutrition, and healing over time.

♡

There’s no need to figure it all out alone. A dietitian and healthcare team can help make a gluten-free plan that works for your health and daily life.
